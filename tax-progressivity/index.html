Progressive or regressive? Design a tax system

A tax is progressive when higher-income households pay a larger share of their income, regressive when they pay a smaller share, and proportional when everyone pays the same share. Washington has no personal income tax and relies heavily on sales tax. Start there, then combine the tools to see how each one changes who pays.

About this model
  • Illustrative, not estimated. The five households stand in for income fifths (quintiles). Their incomes, and the share of income each spends on taxable goods (60% for the lowest, 18% for the highest), are teaching values, not Washington data.
  • Effective rate means total tax divided by annual household income. "Progressive" and "regressive" here describe how that average rate changes as income rises, not the marginal rate on the next dollar.
  • Suits index runs from −1 (all tax paid by the lowest-income group) to +1 (all paid by the highest). A proportional tax scores 0. This model computes it from the five households, each weighted equally.
  • What it leaves out: behavioral responses (people working or buying less), who bears business taxes, property taxes, federal deductibility, and lifetime rather than annual income. Question 4 takes up that last one.
  • Privacy. This page collects no data. It sets no cookies, uses no storage, and makes no network requests after it loads.
  • Accessibility. Every control works by keyboard. The table above gives the same information as the chart, and the summary sentence updates with each change.
