Who votes? Whose voices do elections amplify?

If every adult citizen were equally likely to vote, each group's share of voters would match its share of the population. They don't match. In 1980, Raymond Wolfinger and Steven Rosenstone showed that voters are "a distorted sample that exaggerates the size of some groups and minimizes that of others." Explore their 1972 findings next to recent Cooperative Election Study data, and look for who is overrepresented and who is left out.

Discuss with your group

  1. Wolfinger and Rosenstone concluded that although voters looked different from citizens, their party and policy views were "virtually a carbon copy" of the population in 1972. Look at which groups are most over- and underrepresented today. Would you expect that conclusion to still hold? What would you need to know to test it?
  2. Officials respond to the people who vote. Pick the most underrepresented group on this page. Which public programs or decisions affect that group most, and what might change if its turnout matched its share of citizens?
  3. Some groups are underrepresented because voting is harder for them (moving often, new to the rules), others possibly because they feel less invited in. Which would be easier for a government to fix, and how?
About this data
  • 1972: Raymond E. Wolfinger and Steven J. Rosenstone, Who Votes? (Yale University Press, 1980), Table 6.1, based on the Census Bureau's 1972 Current Population Survey. Citizens over 17; voters in the general election. Puerto Rican, Chicano, and length-of-residence figures were estimated from 1974 data. Turnout in the survey is self-reported.
  • 2020–2024: Cooperative Election Study (CES), validated voters, meaning respondents matched to state voter files rather than relying on self-reports. Values shown now are placeholders; final estimates will come from Prof. Zhao's replication.
  • Comparing eras. Categories don't match exactly. The 1972 age groups are 37–69 and 70–78 where recent data use 37–68 and 69–78, and 1972 education is measured in years of school rather than degrees. Rows that combine eras are marked as approximate.
  • Why a ratio? A group's difference in share (voters minus citizens) depends on how big the group is. The ratio shows how much each member's voice is inflated or deflated: 0.5 means the group casts half the votes its size would predict. The scale is logarithmic so that 0.5 and 2.0 sit equally far from 1.
