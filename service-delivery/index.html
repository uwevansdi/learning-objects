Public Service Delivery Exercise: plan transit for your community

Your group is a planning team for one Seattle community. With limited resources, design a new bus line: decide what your community needs most, choose stations along a route the bus can actually run, and see who can walk to it and which services they can reach.     Before you start: why, what, and how you'll know it's good

    Purpose

    Why we're doing this

    Public services are not distributed equally across communities. Where people live, what resources governments have available, and how public administrators make decisions all influence whether residents can access government services.

    This exercise explores administrative capacity, geographic inequality, and public service delivery, and why the geographic relationship between communities and public services matters for understanding inequality.

    Today, you will take the perspective of a public planning team responsible for designing a new bus route in a Seattle community. Your challenge is to decide how to serve residents with different needs while working with limited resources.

    By completing this exercise, you will practice skills used in public administration and policy analysis:

    • Analyzing evidence: Using maps and demographic information to identify community needs.
    • Evaluating policy tradeoffs: Comparing competing goals, including efficiency, equity, and accessibility.
    • Allocating limited resources: Developing a feasible service plan under budget and infrastructure constraints.
    • Collaborative decision-making: Negotiating among competing priorities and explaining your choices.
    • Adapting to implementation challenges: Revising a policy decision when circumstances change.

    By the end, you should be able to explain why implementing a public service is more complicated than deciding that the service should exist.

    These skills reflect real-world decisions made by government agencies, nonprofit organizations, and our communities.

    Task

    What you'll do, and how

    You are part of a team tasked with designing a new bus line for one Seattle community. Your goal is to develop a feasible plan that considers the different needs of the residents who depend on public transit while working with limited resources.

    You will complete the following steps over 60 minutes:

    1. Community and roles (5 minutes). Review your assigned community and role. Each member should understand their responsibilities before the group begins planning.
    2. Needs and priorities (5 minutes). Examine the map and community information. Identify up to two priorities that will guide your decisions.
    3. Develop your service plan (15 minutes). Select bus stations and construct a route that follows the available street network and meets the resource constraints. Each group member should explain how the plan performs according to their assigned responsibilities.
    4. Implementation challenge (5 minutes). Your team will receive new information that affects its ability to carry out the original plan. Review the challenge and identify which aspects of your proposal are affected.
    5. Revise your plan (12 minutes). Decide how to respond to the new conditions. Compare your revised plan to your original proposal and explain what changed, who benefits, and who might be disadvantaged.
    6. Finalize, export and submit (8 minutes). Complete your group worksheet using your final plan and the reasoning behind your decisions. Make sure the worksheet identifies each element of this exercise. Export your completed work and submit to Canvas.
    7. Class debrief (10 minutes). We will discuss what the exercise reveals about administrative discretion, resource constraints, geography, and equity in public service delivery.

    Working together

    Each group member brings an important perspective, but your final proposal must be a collective decision.

    Listen to the concerns of each role, compare the available evidence, and negotiate when priorities conflict. You do not need unanimous enthusiasm for every decision, but everyone should understand and be able to explain the group's reasoning.

    What makes this difficult?

    You cannot maximize every goal simultaneously. A route that reaches more residents may be longer, more expensive, or less effective at connecting underserved neighborhoods to essential services.

    The implementation challenge will introduce an additional constraint that you cannot fully anticipate. This is intentional as public administrators often adapt policies to changing conditions.

    There is no single correct route. Your task is to develop and defend a reasonable plan.

    Avoid choosing stations solely to maximize one score, ignoring resource constraints, or assuming that all residents have the same transportation needs.

    What happens afterward?

    After the group exercise, your instructor may ask each student to complete an individual reflection connecting your group's decisions to course concepts, such as administrative capacity, discretion, equity, and public service delivery.

    Criteria

    How you'll know you're on track

    The purpose of this exercise is to practice making public service delivery decisions under realistic administrative constraints. There is no single correct solution, and your group is not expected to develop a fully feasible service plan within the allotted time.

    As you work through the exercise, keep the following questions in mind:

    • Community needs: Have we identified our community's most important service-delivery challenges and supported our choices with evidence?
    • Feasibility: Have we made a reasonable effort to develop a plan that accounts for available resources and constraints?
    • Tradeoffs: Can we explain how our choices affect coverage, equitable access, service availability, and travel time?
    • Adaptation: Have we responded to the implementation challenge and explained why we changed or retained our decisions?
    • Communication: Does our completed worksheet clearly explain our reasoning and final proposal?

    These questions are intended to guide your group's discussion and decision-making.

    How your work will be evaluated

    Your instructor will explain how the group worksheet is graded. The emphasis is on completing each part and engaging seriously with the tradeoffs.

    Submission: One 1–2 page Word document per group, submitted through Canvas at the end of the classroom exercise.

    If your course includes an individual reflection, your instructor will post its instructions and due date in Canvas.

    Illustrative example: numbers are fictional.

    "Our original route prioritized total population coverage and connections to major destinations. After the implementation challenge, we revised the route to reduce the number of stations. Our revised plan reaches approximately 1,300 residents instead of 1,500, but maintains connections to a health clinic and food assistance location. We retained a station in a lower-income neighborhood because removing it would have left residents with fewer alternatives for reaching essential services. Although our revised proposal serves fewer people overall, we believe it better balances limited resources, accessibility, and equity."

    Why this is strong: The group identifies the decision, uses quantitative evidence, acknowledges what was lost, and explains why the tradeoff was justified.

      About this exercise and its data
      • Streets and services: © OpenStreetMap contributors, Open Database License. Walking uses streets, stairways, and trails; the bus uses streets only and prefers arterials. Service locations include food banks and pantries, health clinics, and libraries and community centers mapped in OpenStreetMap; coverage is incomplete and should be checked against local directories.
      • Population: American Community Survey 2020–2024 five-year estimates by census block group, prepared by Prof. Zhao: total population, people of color, and people below 200% of the poverty level ("lower income"). Residents are spread evenly along each block group's streets. Block-group estimates have wide margins of error. Where Census measures poverty for fewer than 80% of residents (mostly dorms), the poverty rate isn't shown.
      • Street connectivity: street intersections per square mile, from the U.S. EPA Smart Location Database (2017–2019 data, on 2010 block groups), assigned to 2020 block groups by Prof. Zhao, so neighboring block groups can share a value. A common proxy for walkability, shown for context; it doesn't affect the scorecard.
      • Walking and travel: a 10-minute walk is 800 meters along the street network on flat ground, and less on hills. Walking time on each street follows Tobler's hiking function on U.S. Geological Survey elevation data (1 arc-second, about 30 m), averaged over walking uphill and down; bridges count as level. Trip times assume an average bus speed of 18 km/h including stops.
      • Candidate stations sit about every kilometer along each named arterial, at a cross street, chosen automatically. Instructors may replace them with their own list.
      • Privacy. This page collects no data. Group names and notes stay in this browser tab and go only into the summary you copy or download.
      • Accessibility. Every step works by keyboard. Stations can be added, removed, and reordered from the lists; the scorecard states every result in text.
